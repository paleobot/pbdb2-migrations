A field guide to pbdb2, part two

The Containment Ledger

How the next Paleobiology Database decides what belongs inside what — across two family trees the old system never actually kept apart, and the combined view that now sits above both.

The companion page to this one covers identity: how the system decides what a name is — a card, a lineage, a concept, an accepted spelling. This page assumes you've read that and moves to the other half of the question: what a name belongs to, and whether "belongs to" even means the same thing for every name.

It doesn't. A name published with a rank — genus, family, order — sits in one hierarchy. A name published as an "unranked clade," the modern habit of naming a group without committing to where it sits on the old Linnaean ladder, sits in a second, separate hierarchy. This page used to be about why those two hierarchies are kept apart forever, with a narrow bridge built between them. It no longer is: a third, primary ledger now resolves both together, and the two separate ledgers described below are its secondary, supporting layer. This page covers all of it — the two-ledger split that came first, the bridge between them, and the combined view built on top — through three real cases checked against live data.

The big idea

Two family trees, a bridge between them — and now, a combined view above both

Linnaean ranks and modern unranked clades are two different ways of naming a group. This system keeps a secondary ledger for each — and now derives a primary, combined hierarchy directly from the same underlying opinions, resolving the two together instead of asking a caller to reconcile them by hand.

The identity guide's Linnaean ledger — taxa_linnaean — holds every name accepted at a genuine Linnaean rank: kingdom down to subspecies. Its containment column, containing_concept_permid, is filled in by the exact same recompute step that decides accepted spellings and synonyms, just answering a different question: given every "belongs to" claim ever filed about a concept, which one wins?

Names published as an unranked clade — Archosauria, Pancrustacea, whole modern chunks of the tree of life that working systematists name without ranking — get their own, parallel ledger: taxa_clades. It runs the identical machinery, on the identical rule, restricted to candidates where both sides of a claim are themselves unranked. Within these two ledgers, a clade can contain another clade, and a Linnaean concept can contain another Linnaean concept — but neither can cross into the other's tree.

Every real "belongs to" claim that crosses that line — a genus placed inside an unranked clade, or an unranked clade placed inside a Linnaean order — still exists in the source opinions. It's these two ledgers' own pooling that refuses it, by design: each one only ever considers candidates from its own side. A third ledger, taxa_attachments, exists specifically to keep every cross-boundary claim as an explicit, queryable fact — a concept can carry more than one, since the claims never actually compete against each other in that ledger.

All three of those remain exactly as before, unchanged in behavior. What's new is a fourth, primary ledger — taxa — that resolves Linnaean and clade opinions together, from scratch, with no boundary at all. It doesn't read the other three; it re-derives the whole hierarchy from the same underlying opinions in one pass, so a claim that crosses the old line competes on equal footing with everything else, and wins or loses the ordinary way. taxa_linnaean, taxa_clades, and taxa_attachments remain as the secondary, per-domain record; taxa is now the one to read for a single best answer.

How a container gets chosen — the two secondary ledgers

Borrow from a synonym, never invert a rank, never cross the wall

taxa_linnaean and taxa_clades fill in their containment column the same way: pool every placement claim across a concept's synonyms, then exclude the ones that can't possibly be right before anything else is even weighed. (The combined taxa ledger reuses this same pooling and tie-breaking rule too — the next section covers exactly where it differs.)

A concept's container isn't just whatever the accepted name's own placement claim says. Every synonym filed under that concept gets a vote too — a claim filed under a junior synonym is allowed to win if it's better evidenced or more recent than anything filed directly on the accepted name. This is deliberate: a specialist's paper about an obscure synonym can still carry real information about where the whole group belongs. The one exception is species — a species is placed by its own direct claim only, never borrowed from a synonym, because a species has to sit in its current genus regardless of what's argued about a synonym somewhere else.

Two kinds of candidates get thrown out before the ranking contest even starts, no matter how well-evidenced they look. A claim that would make a concept its own container — a leftover placement filed before two names were recognized as synonyms of each other — is excluded outright. And on the Linnaean side only, a claim is excluded if the proposed container is more finely ranked than the concept itself: a family cannot be placed inside its own subfamily. Equal rank is fine — one genus can legitimately sit inside another — it's only an inversion that's barred.

Neither of these two ledgers ever lets a claim cross the boundary between them through this normal path. An unranked clade is never assigned a Linnaean container in taxa_linnaean, and it's never borrowed as one either — taxa_clades runs its own copy of this same pooling logic, restricted the other way, to candidates where both sides are unranked. A claim that names one side ranked and the other unranked doesn't win or lose in either pool. It's simply not a candidate in either — which is exactly the gap taxa_attachments exists to fill, and exactly the gap the combined taxa ledger resolves a different way, covered next.

How a container gets chosen — the combined primary ledger

taxa: the same rule, with the wall taken down

The combined ledger doesn't read taxa_linnaean, taxa_clades, or taxa_attachments and stitch them together. It re-derives the whole hierarchy from the same underlying opinions in one pass, with the Linnaean/clade boundary removed from every step — spelling grouping, synonym grouping, and containment pooling alike.

Concretely, that means a genus's synonym can be an unranked clade, and the merge is allowed; an unranked clade's container can be a Linnaean order, and the pooling is allowed; a family and an unranked clade with a shared, tangled opinion history can turn out to be the same concept, once nothing is stopping the merge from completing. Case 3 below is a real example of exactly that last kind.

One exclusion from the Linnaean side survives, in a modified form: a container still can't be more finely ranked than what it contains. But "finely ranked" has no meaning for an unranked clade — there's no coarser-or-finer ordering between two clades, or between a clade and a rank, the way there is between two ranks. So the combined ledger checks this only when both sides have a real rank to compare; a candidate touching an unranked clade on either side skips the check entirely, rather than being silently excluded the way it would be under the old wall. Nothing else from the old exclusion list survives — a concept accepted as an unranked clade can now have a container of its own, and can now serve as another concept's container, both for the first time.

Taking the wall down does exactly what it sounds like it would: it lets real disagreements that used to be structurally impossible actually surface, including genuine containment cycles between a Linnaean concept and a clade concept — a shape neither taxa_linnaean nor taxa_clades could ever produce on its own, since each excludes the other's side entirely. The combined ledger resolves those the same way taxa_clades already resolves clade-only cycles: cut the single weakest candidate among a cycle's own members, recompute, repeat until none remain. It never raises an error over a cycle — only if that loop somehow fails to terminate at all. Checked against real data, this happens more, and at a different scale, than either separate ledger ever saw: dropping the wall surfaced a handful of real disagreements among a few major, deep-history clade names sitting near the root of the entire tree of life, each resolved the same way, cut by cut.

Breaking ties

The same three questions, run independently in each ledger

Once the impossible candidates are gone, every ledger — all three secondary ones, and the combined primary ledger above them — settles a contest the identical way: the same rule the identity guide describes for spellings and synonyms, run again here for containment.

FIRST

Was evidence given?

A claim backed by an argument outranks one that's just asserted.

THEN

Which is newer?

Among equally-argued claims, the most recent publication wins.

FINALLY

Which was entered later?

A last-resort tiebreaker for an exact tie on both counts.

That's the whole rule, and it runs independently in taxa_linnaean, taxa_clades, taxa_attachments, and taxa alike, each strictly within its own candidate pool. Nothing about the rule itself differs between them. What differs is what happens when the pooled candidates form a genuine cycle: concept A's winning claim points to B, and B's, however indirectly, points back to A.

On the Linnaean side, the rank-inversion exclusion above does most of the work of preventing a cycle from ever forming — a coarser thing can never be placed inside a finer one, which rules out most loops before they start. On the rare occasion a genuine cycle still forms among concepts of compatible rank, taxa_linnaean resolves it the same way the clade side always has: it finds every concept whose own containment chain loops back to itself, cuts the single weakest candidate among just those concepts, and repeats until no cycle remains, rather than guessing or leaving a partial answer. It only raises an error if that loop somehow fails to settle after a very large number of rounds — not merely because a cycle was found.

Unranked clades have no rank-based firewall at all — there's no "coarser or finer" between two unranked names, so nothing structural stops a cycle from forming on the clade side the way rank inversion mostly does on the Linnaean side. taxa_clades resolves a genuine cycle the identical way: cuts the single weakest candidate — lowest evidence, then oldest, then earliest-entered — among just the looping concepts, and repeats. Every cut, on either side, is permanently recorded and queryable afterward, not just inspectable within the session that computed it.

One wrinkle sits ahead of that last "earliest-entered" step: a candidate that only reached a concept because an unrelated name was pooled in as its synonym is cut before one filed directly on the concept's own anchor name, if the two are otherwise tied. A synonym-pooling artifact is a more likely explanation for a tie than a real disagreement between two carefully-argued claims, so it loses first. That's genuinely useful — Case 1 below shows it working correctly — but it only breaks that specific kind of tie. Two directly-filed, equally senior, equally well-evidenced claims from different concepts still fall through to the same arbitrary last resort as everywhere else in the system, and neither the clade side nor the combined ledger has a rank-based ordering to stop every possible cycle from forming in the first place. The tiebreak narrows the risk; it doesn't remove it — and the combined ledger, having no boundary left to shrink the candidate pool at all, sees this kind of cycle more often than either secondary ledger did on its own.

Worked examples

Case files

Three real cases, each checked directly against the old system's own live data and, for two of them, against both the secondary ledgers and the new combined one — not described from memory or invented for the page: a place a genuine tie gets broken correctly, a place a real cost has now been resolved, and a place the combined ledger surfaces something neither older ledger could.

Case 1clade cycle · a genuine tie, correctly broken

Ornithopoda and Clypeodonta

Real data, checked against both the old system's live database and the new system's current derived output.

Every opinion ever filed about this pair — three of them, the most recent backed by stated evidence — says the same thing: Clypeodonta belongs to Ornithopoda. Not one of them says otherwise. The old system's own live classification agrees: Clypeodonta sits under Ornithopoda, which in turn sits under Cerapoda — an uncontested placement matching how ornithischian dinosaurs are usually grouped. (Ornithopoda itself has been filed at five different formal ranks over its history — suborder, order, superfamily, infraorder, and finally unranked clade — which is exactly the kind of history the identity guide's lineage mechanism exists to sort out; only the lineage's one accepted rank matters here.)

The new system agrees too — but it's a closer call than the unanimous opinions suggest. Pooling reached a separate, unrelated, unevidenced opinion into Ornithopoda's concept from one of its synonyms, and that pooled candidate tied on evidence and year with Clypeodonta's own direct opinion. The tie-breaking rule from the previous section exists exactly for this situation: it cuts the candidate that only arrived through pooling, leaving Clypeodonta's own directly-filed opinion standing. Ornithopoda resolves under Cerapoda, Clypeodonta resolves under Ornithopoda — matching the old system exactly.

The point: a synonym-pooling artifact and a concept's own real evidence can end up looking identical on the usual scorecard. Preferring to cut the pooled candidate on a tie is what keeps that kind of coincidence from deciding the outcome — but it's a narrow fix for a narrow problem. It doesn't cover every possible tie, and the clade side still has no rank-based ordering to prevent a cycle from forming in the first place.
Case 2cross-boundary · a real cost, now resolved

Bredocaris: one answer again, and it's the right one

Real data, checked against the old system's live database and against all four of the new system's ledgers.

Exactly two opinions have ever been filed about this small arthropod genus: "belongs to Orstenocarida" — an order-level placement, no evidence stated, filed under a reference the old system's own reliability rule treats as the weakest kind on record — and "belongs to Pancrustacea" — an unranked clade, backed by stated evidence. The old system ranks every opinion in one pool regardless of category, so it correctly picks Pancrustacea as Bredocaris's one classification. The weaker Orstenocarida opinion is still on file, but nothing in the old system's own display ever shows it once it loses.

taxa_linnaean still shows the cost this page originally described: because its container is chosen from Linnaean-only candidates and Pancrustacea is cross-boundary, it resolves Bredocaris to Orstenocarida — the weaker opinion — while taxa_attachments separately, correctly, records the better-evidenced Pancrustacea placement. Reading taxa_linnaean alone still gives the less-supported answer, exactly as before.

The combined taxa ledger doesn't have that problem, because it isn't choosing from a restricted pool in the first place: both opinions compete directly, and Pancrustacea wins on its own merits — better evidence, no boundary to sit on the wrong side of. Checked against the current derived output, that's exactly what it shows.

The point: this was never a limitation of the evidence-ranking rule itself — it was a rule applied to an artificially narrowed pool. The combined ledger applies the identical rule to the full pool, and the better-evidenced opinion wins the way it always should have. taxa_attachments still keeps the Pancrustacea placement visible as its own explicit, queryable fact, same as before — it's just no longer the only place the right answer for Bredocaris shows up.
Case 3cross-boundary · what looked like alternatives turns out to be one answer

Wiwaxia: two "coexisting hypotheses" that were never actually two

Real data, checked against the old system's live database and against all four of the new system's ledgers.

Wiwaxia is a famously contested Cambrian fossil, and its opinion history shows it: six placements on file, in order — Sachitida (an order-level grouping, no evidence stated), Halwaxida (an unranked clade, stated evidence, published 2007 — the old system's current cached answer), Annelida (a phylum, no evidence stated), Metazoa (a subkingdom, second-hand), and Wiwaxidae twice, the second time with stated evidence but citing a much older (1911) publication. taxa_linnaean shows Wiwaxidae as the current answer — not because it's actually the best-evidenced opinion on file, but because Halwaxida, an unranked clade, was never a candidate for a Linnaean concept's container in the first place. Separately, taxa_attachments keeps the Sachitida and Annelida placements on record as their own explicit, cross-boundary facts — exactly as this page originally described, and exactly what made this look like a case of several genuine, coexisting hypotheses sitting side by side.

The combined taxa ledger doesn't have a rank wall to sit on the wrong side of, so it just applies the ordinary evidence rule to Wiwaxia's full opinion history: Halwaxida's 2007 evidenced opinion outranks Wiwaxidae's 1911 one, the same way it always should have. That much matches the old system's own current answer. But Halwaxida turns out to already be a junior synonym of Sachitida — a merge taxa_clades has independently derived for some time, unrelated to anything new here — and the combined ledger additionally recognizes Thambetolepida as a junior synonym of the same senior name, a real, evidenced opinion that could never merge across the old wall before now. Sachitida itself has never been asserted junior to anything on file, so it wins the concept's senior name outright. Wiwaxia's combined-ledger classification therefore reads as "Sachitida" — not a different container than Halwaxida, but the correct senior name for the identical one.

The point: one of the two "coexisting hypotheses" taxa_attachments preserves for this taxon — Sachitida — was never actually a competing alternative to the current answer. It's a synonym of it, hiding in plain sight because the old rank wall kept the two names permanently apart. Annelida remains a genuinely separate, weaker, still-on-file hypothesis; that part of the original story is unchanged.
For readers who know the old system

Four ledgers, not one — and the primary one now matches the old system's own ambition

The old system has no separate concept of a clade hierarchy, or of a cross-boundary attachment, at all. An unranked clade there is just another row in one undifferentiated tree, with its usual rank-order sanity check quietly switched off rather than routed anywhere different. This redesign's two secondary ledgers plus a bridge were the first cut at that problem; the combined primary ledger is the second, and it closes a gap the first cut deliberately left open.

Splitting the two hierarchies apart is what makes taxa_linnaean and taxa_clades each internally clean — a Linnaean concept and a clade concept can never merge into each other or cycle through each other within either one, however tangled either side's own opinions get. That isolation is also exactly what makes Case 1's situation possible: with no rank ordering left on the clade side to catch a cycle before it forms, telling a real disagreement apart from a synonym-pooling artifact falls entirely on the tie-breaking rule. Today that rule gets it right, for the specific kind of tie it was built to catch — but it's a narrower guarantee than the rank-based firewall the Linnaean side gets for free.

Case 2 and Case 3 are a different story than they used to be. Both used to illustrate a real, recurring cost of the two-ledger split: evidence stopped competing across the boundary the old system always ranked as one pool, so the "main" Linnaean answer could be weaker than — or blind to a synonymy with — something sitting right next to it in the bridge table. The combined taxa ledger removes that cost directly, by removing the boundary that caused it, rather than by adding a second place to look. taxa_linnaean, taxa_clades, and taxa_attachments still exist, still behave exactly as before, and still matter — they're what the combined ledger's own containment cycles get compared against, and they're the finer-grained, per-domain record when someone specifically needs "the Linnaean-only view" or "the clade-only view" rather than the resolved one. But for the question the old system's one pool always answered — what's this taxon's single, best-evidenced classification, full stop — taxa is now the ledger that answers it, the same way Classic's one slot always tried to.

None of this is free. Resolving Linnaean and clade opinions together, with no boundary to shrink the candidate pool, means the combined ledger sees containment cycles the two secondary ledgers structurally couldn't — checked against the full current dataset, a real one turned up among a handful of major, deep-history clade names sitting near the root of the entire tree of life, resolved the same cut-the-weakest-edge way described above. It's also slower to compute in full: rebuilding the combined ledger from scratch currently takes on the order of minutes, not the roughly-twenty-seconds the Linnaean-only ledger alone needs, because each cycle it resolves means rebuilding its full candidate pool again. Both are known, monitored trade-offs of resolving everything together rather than an oversight this page is glossing over.

Where this stands today All four ledgers — taxa_linnaean, taxa_clades, taxa_attachments, and the combined taxa — are implemented and validated end-to-end against the full real, migrated dataset, including a genuine cross-boundary containment cycle the combined ledger had to learn to resolve (see above). This isn't a design sketch the way parts of the identity guide are.
