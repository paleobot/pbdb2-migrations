A field guide to pbdb2, part two

The Containment Ledger

How the next Paleobiology Database decides what belongs inside what — across two family trees the old system never actually kept apart.

The companion page to this one covers identity: how the system decides what a name is — a card, a lineage, a concept, an accepted spelling. This page assumes you've read that and moves to the other half of the question: what a name belongs to, and whether "belongs to" even means the same thing for every name.

It doesn't. A name published with a rank — genus, family, order — sits in one hierarchy. A name published as an "unranked clade," the modern habit of naming a group without committing to where it sits on the old Linnaean ladder, sits in a second, separate hierarchy. This page is about why those two hierarchies are kept apart, the narrow bridge built between them, and three real cases — checked against the old system's actual, live behavior — of what that separation gets right, what it costs, and where it still needs work.

The big idea

Two family trees, joined by an explicit bridge

Linnaean ranks and modern unranked clades are two different ways of naming a group. The redesign gives each its own ledger, rather than pretending they're one tree with a few odd branches.

The identity guide's ledger — taxa — holds every name accepted at a genuine Linnaean rank: kingdom down to subspecies. Its containment column, containing_concept_permid, is filled in by the exact same recompute step that decides accepted spellings and synonyms, just answering a different question: given every "belongs to" claim ever filed about a concept, which one wins?

Names published as an unranked clade — Archosauria, Pancrustacea, whole modern chunks of the tree of life that working systematists name without ranking — get their own, parallel ledger: taxa_clades. It runs the identical machinery, on the identical rule, restricted to candidates where both sides of a claim are themselves unranked. A clade can contain another clade. It cannot, through this path, contain or be contained by a Linnaean concept — that boundary is a hard wall, not a preference.

Every real "belongs to" claim that crosses the wall — a genus placed inside an unranked clade, or an unranked clade placed inside a Linnaean order — still exists in the source opinions. It's the two trees above that refuse it, by design: each one's pooling only ever considers candidates from its own side, so a cross-boundary claim can't win either contest no matter how well-evidenced it is. Before this bridge existed, that exclusion meant those claims were weighed by nothing at all — not beaten fairly, just never in the running anywhere. The redesign gives them a third, explicit home instead: clade_attachments, a bridge table built specifically for cross-boundary claims, and only for those.

◭

taxa

The Linnaean tree. One container per concept, chosen by pooling every synonym's placement claims at equal rank.

◬

The bridge. Every claim that crosses the wall, in either direction — and, unlike the other two, a concept can have more than one.

How a container gets chosen

Borrow from a synonym, never invert a rank, never cross the wall

Both trees fill in their containment column the same way: pool every placement claim across a concept's synonyms, then exclude the ones that can't possibly be right before anything else is even weighed.

A concept's container isn't just whatever the accepted name's own placement claim says. Every synonym filed under that concept gets a vote too — a claim filed under a junior synonym is allowed to win if it's better evidenced or more recent than anything filed directly on the accepted name. This is deliberate: a specialist's paper about an obscure synonym can still carry real information about where the whole group belongs. The one exception is species — a species is placed by its own direct claim only, never borrowed from a synonym, because a species has to sit in its current genus regardless of what's argued about a synonym somewhere else.

Two kinds of candidates get thrown out before the ranking contest even starts, no matter how well-evidenced they look. A claim that would make a concept its own container — a leftover placement filed before two names were recognized as synonyms of each other — is excluded outright. And on the Linnaean side only, a claim is excluded if the proposed container is more finely ranked than the concept itself: a family cannot be placed inside its own subfamily. Equal rank is fine — one genus can legitimately sit inside another — it's only an inversion that's barred.

Neither tree ever lets a claim cross the boundary between them through this normal path. An unranked clade is never assigned a Linnaean container, and it's never borrowed as one either — taxa_clades runs its own copy of this same pooling logic, restricted the other way, to candidates where both sides are unranked. A claim that names one side ranked and the other unranked doesn't win or lose in either pool. It's simply not a candidate in either — which is exactly the gap clade_attachments exists to fill.

Breaking ties

The same three questions, run independently in each ledger

Once the impossible candidates are gone, all three ledgers settle a contest the identical way — the same rule the identity guide describes for spellings and synonyms, run again here for containment.

FIRST

Was evidence given?

A claim backed by an argument outranks one that's just asserted.

THEN

Which is newer?

Among equally-argued claims, the most recent publication wins.

FINALLY

Which was entered later?

A last-resort tiebreaker for an exact tie on both counts.

That's the whole rule, and it runs three separate times — once for taxa, once for taxa_clades, once for clade_attachments — each strictly within its own candidate pool. Nothing about the rule itself differs between the Linnaean and clade sides. What differs is what happens when the pooled candidates form a genuine cycle: concept A's winning claim points to B, and B's, however indirectly, points back to A.

On the Linnaean side, the rank-inversion exclusion above does most of the work of preventing a cycle from ever forming — a coarser thing can never be placed inside a finer one, which rules out most loops before they start. On the rare occasion a genuine cycle still forms among concepts of compatible rank, taxa's derivation stops and raises an error identifying it, rather than guessing.

Unranked clades have no such firewall. There's no "coarser or finer" between two unranked names — that ordering doesn't exist for them at all — so nothing structural stops a cycle from forming on the clade side the way rank inversion does on the Linnaean side. Rather than block the entire derivation over it, taxa_clades resolves a genuine cycle automatically: it finds every concept whose own containment chain loops back to itself, cuts the single weakest candidate — lowest evidence, then oldest, then earliest-entered — among just those concepts, and repeats until no cycle remains. Every cut is permanently recorded and queryable afterward, not just inspectable within the session that computed it.

One wrinkle sits ahead of that last "earliest-entered" step: a candidate that only reached a concept because an unrelated name was pooled in as its synonym is cut before one filed directly on the concept's own anchor name, if the two are otherwise tied. A synonym-pooling artifact is a more likely explanation for a tie than a real disagreement between two carefully-argued claims, so it loses first. That's genuinely useful — Case 1 below shows it working correctly — but it only breaks that specific kind of tie. Two directly-filed, equally senior, equally well-evidenced claims from different concepts still fall through to the same arbitrary last resort as everywhere else in the system, and the clade side still has no rank-based ordering to stop a cycle from forming in the first place. The tiebreak narrows the risk; it doesn't remove it.

Worked examples

Case files

Three real cases, each checked directly against the old system's own live data rather than described from memory or invented for the page: a place a genuine tie gets broken correctly, a place the design trades one gain for one cost, and a place it's unambiguously better.

Case 1clade cycle · a genuine tie, correctly broken

Ornithopoda and Clypeodonta

Real data, checked against both the old system's live database and the new system's current derived output.

Every opinion ever filed about this pair — three of them, the most recent backed by stated evidence — says the same thing: Clypeodonta belongs to Ornithopoda. Not one of them says otherwise. The old system's own live classification agrees: Clypeodonta sits under Ornithopoda, which in turn sits under Cerapoda — an uncontested placement matching how ornithischian dinosaurs are usually grouped. (Ornithopoda itself has been filed at five different formal ranks over its history — suborder, order, superfamily, infraorder, and finally unranked clade — which is exactly the kind of history the identity guide's lineage mechanism exists to sort out; only the lineage's one accepted rank matters here.)

The new system agrees too — but it's a closer call than the unanimous opinions suggest. Pooling reached a separate, unrelated, unevidenced opinion into Ornithopoda's concept from one of its synonyms, and that pooled candidate tied on evidence and year with Clypeodonta's own direct opinion. The tie-breaking rule from the previous section exists exactly for this situation: it cuts the candidate that only arrived through pooling, leaving Clypeodonta's own directly-filed opinion standing. Ornithopoda resolves under Cerapoda, Clypeodonta resolves under Ornithopoda — matching the old system exactly.

The point: a synonym-pooling artifact and a concept's own real evidence can end up looking identical on the usual scorecard. Preferring to cut the pooled candidate on a tie is what keeps that kind of coincidence from deciding the outcome — but it's a narrow fix for a narrow problem. It doesn't cover every possible tie, and the clade side still has no rank-based ordering to prevent a cycle from forming in the first place.
Case 2cross-boundary · a real trade-off

Bredocaris: two containers, not one

Real data, checked against both the old system's live database and the new system's current derived output.

Exactly two opinions have ever been filed about this small arthropod genus: "belongs to Orstenocarida" — an order-level placement, no evidence stated, filed under a reference the old system's own reliability rule treats as the weakest kind on record — and "belongs to Pancrustacea" — an unranked clade, backed by stated evidence. The old system ranks every opinion in one pool regardless of category, so it correctly picks Pancrustacea as Bredocaris's one classification. The weaker Orstenocarida opinion is still on file, but nothing in the old system's own display ever shows it once it loses.

The new system keeps both opinions visible — but not by picking the better one. Because a Linnaean concept's container is chosen from Linnaean-only candidates, and Pancrustacea is cross-boundary, Bredocaris's main classification in taxa resolves to Orstenocarida — the weaker opinion — while the better-evidenced Pancrustacea placement sits, correctly, in clade_attachments. The two claims never actually compete against each other; they're ranked in separate pools.

The gain: nothing is silently discarded the way the losing opinion is in the old system — both claims survive as explicit, queryable facts.
The cost: there is currently no single answer to "what's Bredocaris's best-evidenced classification, full stop." Reading taxa alone gives a less-supported answer than the old system's own single ranked pool did for the identical question — finding the better one means knowing to also check clade_attachments.
Case 3cross-boundary · a real win

Wiwaxia's coexisting hypotheses

Real data, checked against both the old system's live database and the new system's current derived output.

Wiwaxia is a famously contested Cambrian fossil, and its opinion history shows it: six placements on file, in order — Sachitida (an order-level grouping, no evidence stated), Halwaxida (an unranked clade, stated evidence — the old system's current cached answer), Annelida (a phylum, no evidence stated), Metazoa (a subkingdom, second-hand), and Wiwaxidae twice, the second time with stated evidence and the most recent entry on file. The old system has room for exactly one answer at a time. It currently shows Halwaxida — which means the historically real Sachitida and Annelida hypotheses, and arguably the more current Wiwaxidae opinion, are invisible in the classification itself. Finding out they were ever seriously argued means reading the raw opinion history by hand.

The new system's taxa table gets the current answer right — Wiwaxidae, matching the newest, best-evidenced opinion on file. And separately, clade_attachments keeps both the Sachitida and Annelida placements on record as live, labeled facts, not buried history. All three coexist because none of them are actually competing: each is judged against its own specific target, not against each other.

The point: no hedge needed here. The current answer is correct, and real information about a genuinely, repeatedly re-argued taxon survives that the old system's one-container-per-taxon design could never hold onto, for any taxon, by construction.
For readers who know the old system

Not simply better. Structured differently, on purpose.

The old system has no separate concept of a clade hierarchy, or of a cross-boundary attachment, at all. An unranked clade there is just another row in one undifferentiated tree, with its usual rank-order sanity check quietly switched off rather than routed anywhere different. The redesign's three-ledger split is a real trade, not a strict upgrade — the three cases above show all of it, not just the flattering part.

Splitting the two hierarchies apart is what makes taxa and taxa_clades each internally clean — a Linnaean concept and a clade concept can never merge into each other or cycle through each other, however tangled either side's own opinions get. That isolation is also exactly what makes Case 1's situation possible: with no rank ordering left on the clade side to catch a cycle before it forms, telling a real disagreement apart from a synonym-pooling artifact falls entirely on the tie-breaking rule. Today that rule gets it right, for the specific kind of tie it was built to catch — but it's a narrower guarantee than the rank-based firewall the Linnaean side gets for free, and the same isolation produces Case 2's recurring cost: evidence no longer competes across the boundary the old system always ranked as one pool, so the "main" answer in taxa can be weaker than the one sitting right next to it in clade_attachments.

But the same isolation is what makes Case 3 possible at all. The old system's single classification slot per taxon means a genuinely contested name — one the literature has placed in three or four different groups over its history — can only ever show the current winner, with everything else invisible unless someone goes looking. The redesign's separate, explicit bridge table means a well-evidenced alternative placement doesn't have to lose a fight it was never actually in to stay visible.

What's missing today, on both sides of that comparison, is a single combined view that ranks a Linnaean and a cross-boundary claim against each other directly — the way the old system's one pool always did, for better and for worse. Building that view is a deliberate piece of future work, not an oversight this page is glossing over.

Where this stands today taxa_clades and clade_attachments are implemented and validated against real, migrated data — this isn't a design sketch the way parts of the identity guide are. The OpenSpec change that built them is complete but not yet archived as of this writing.
