A field guide to pbdb2

The Taxonomy Ledger

How the next Paleobiology Database keeps track of fossil names — and decides, on its own, what everyone currently believes.

In the old system, a curator typed in a paper's opinion about a name, and once a day a slow background job re-read every opinion ever entered and rebuilt its best guess at "the" classification. The new system does the same job — but the bookkeeping is simpler, the answer is always current, and nothing is ever silently overwritten.

This page walks through the idea from scratch. No screenshots, no click-by-click steps — the data-entry screens for this system haven't been built yet. What follows is the model those screens will eventually sit on top of.

The big idea

Notes go in. A ledger comes out. Nobody edits the ledger.

Everything in this system rests on keeping two things strictly separate: what a published paper said, and what the database currently believes.

Think of every published taxonomic claim — "this species belongs in that genus," "these two names are the same animal," "this name was misspelled" — as a small note clipped to an index card. Nobody ever deletes a note, even a note from a hundred years ago that everyone now disagrees with. It's part of the historical record: someone, somewhere, published that claim.

A separate, unwritten-on ledger holds the current answer for every name: is it accepted, what does it belong to, is it a synonym of something else. Nobody fills the ledger in by hand. It's entirely recomputed from the notes — the same way a librarian re-derives "the current consensus" by reading through a card's whole file rather than trusting whatever's scrawled on the outside of the folder.

That's the whole trick. If the ledger and the notes ever disagree, the notes win, and the ledger gets rebuilt. There is no "correct" version living only in someone's head or in a value nobody wrote down where it came from.

The unit of identity

Every exact spelling gets its own card, forever

The single most important rule in the new system: a card's name and rank never change. If either changes, that's a different card.

Once a card is written — say, Roveacrinus alata, species — nobody ever edits its name field. If a later paper decides the ending should be -us instead of -a to match Latin grammar, that correction doesn't touch the old card. It creates a brand-new card, Roveacrinus alatus, and a note links the two together: "this is a corrected form of that."

Follow enough of those linking notes — corrections, regenderings, rank changes, genus reassignments — and you get a whole chain of cards that are all, at bottom, the same published name going through its history of respellings. The system calls that a lineage, and it picks one card from the chain to be the one currently in use.

What gets clipped to a card

Three kinds of notes, three different jobs

Every published taxonomic claim is one of exactly three kinds. Keeping them apart is what makes the recompute step tractable.

Spelling & sameness

Is this card a corrected, respelled, or renamed form of another card? Or is it a wholly different name for the same animal — a synonym?

e.g. "alata is a correction of alatus" · "scalper is a synonym of sulcatus"

⊂

Placement

What higher group does this name belong to? A species in a genus, a genus in a family — one card containing another.

e.g. "Dorudon belongs to Basilosauridae"

?

Status

A note purely about the name's own standing — is it too poorly known to use, or was it published without following the rules?

e.g. "nomen dubium" · "nomen nudum"

Placement notes are read a little differently from the other two — but only above the species level. For a genus or higher, a synonym doesn't lose its own placement claims just because it's no longer the "main" name — the system pools placement notes across every name judged to be the same taxon, and lets the best-evidenced one win, even if it happens to be filed under a junior synonym. That mirrors how a working taxonomist actually reasons about where something belongs. At the species level, though, only the accepted name's own placement notes are read; a junior synonym's containment claims stay filed under that synonym and are never borrowed.

Breaking ties

When notes disagree, three questions settle it

Two papers can make opposite claims about the same name. The recompute step needs one deterministic way to pick a winner — every time, for every kind of note.

FIRST

Was evidence given?

A claim backed by an argument — a described character, an analysis — outranks a claim that's just asserted.

THEN

Which is newer?

Among equally well-argued claims, the most recently published one reflects the current state of the science.

FINALLY

Which was entered later?

A last-resort tiebreaker for the rare case where evidence and year both tie exactly.

That's it — the same three questions, asked in the same order, for spelling, for placement, and for status, independently. Merging two names into one concept (Case 3, next) runs the identical three questions too, just with one extra check ahead of them: whichever name was never itself accused of being the junior one gets first refusal, before evidence even gets asked. There's no other special-case logic hiding anywhere, and no daily batch job: the moment a new note is entered, this ranking runs and the ledger is brought current. There's no overnight wait to see whether a name's classification changed.

Worked examples

Case files

Four examples, each adding one more piece of the mechanism. The first two are drawn from real published names; the last two use a well-known genus-confusion case to walk through what a reversal looks like.

Case 1spelling · correction

Roveacrinus alata → alatus

A grammatical correction, matching the species epithet's ending to standard Latin usage.

A card exists for Roveacrinus alata. A later paper corrects the ending to -us. That correction is entered as its own card, linked back to the original with a "correction of" note — not an edit to the first card.

Both cards are eligible to be the lineage's current spelling. Since the corrected form is the newer, better-argued claim, it wins the ranking and becomes the name actually shown as current — while the original spelling stays on record, permanently, as part of the same lineage.

The point: a correction is a competition between two eligible cards. The newer one usually wins, but it has to earn that — nothing about "being a correction" makes it automatic.
Case 2spelling · misspelling

Myliobatus vs. Myliobatis

Illustrative — a classic textbook case of a one-letter genus confusion in eagle rays.

Suppose a paper introduces the spelling Myliobatus. A later paper points out this is simply a misspelling of the already-established genus Myliobatis — not a new name, not a correction of a typo in the Latin grammar, just an error.

That note links the two cards, exactly like Case 1's correction note does. But it's flagged differently: a misspelling is marked so that it can never win the "current spelling" ranking, no matter how much evidence or how many years pass in its favor. A correction is a fair competitor; a misspelling is disqualified from the race entirely.

The point: not every linking note makes its card eligible to become the accepted name. Misspellings are linked into the lineage — so searches and downloads still find them — but they can't win it.
Case 3sameness · synonymy

Taeniolabis scalper is a junior synonym of Taeniolabis sulcatus

Cope named the same specimen twice: sulcatus in 1882, then scalper in 1884.

These aren't the same spelling of one name — they're two entirely different, independently published names that turned out to describe the same animal. A "same taxon as" note merges their two lineages into one group the system calls a concept.

Merging picks a winner with one extra rule ahead of the usual three: whichever name was never itself accused of being the junior side of a currently-standing synonymy note gets first refusal. Here the note runs "scalper is a junior synonym of sulcatus" — never the reverse — so sulcatus wins outright on that check alone, before evidence, recency, or even publication year are ever consulted. sulcatus becomes the concept's name, and scalper folds in permanently as a recorded junior synonym.

(When several names are tangled in a genuine three-or-more-way synonymy and no single one is unambiguously "the senior," the same evidence-then-recency contest from the previous section breaks the tie — publication year only gets the final word if evidence and recency are exactly equal too.)

The point: a lineage groups spellings of one published name. A concept groups multiple, separately published names that turned out to be the same taxon. They're different groupings, decided by different rules.
Case 4sameness · reversal

Un-merging Myliobatus and Myliobatis

Continuing Case 2 — a specialist re-examines the type material and disagrees with the misspelling claim.

Say a later, more thorough study argues Case 2 had it backwards: Myliobatus isn't a misspelling at all, but a distinct, valid genus. In the old system, undoing a merge like this meant walking back through the ledger's history to find the last state before the bad claim took hold — a genuinely difficult piece of code, prone to breaking on edge cases.

Here, undoing is just doing — but it takes a specific kind of note, not just any new one. The specialist's paper is entered as a direct rejection of the earlier claim — "Myliobatus is not a misspelling of Myliobatis," with its own evidence and year — rather than a free-floating comment that the two are unrelated. That note competes in the exact same evidence-then-recency-then-entry-order contest as every other note about Myliobatus's spelling. If it wins, the 1990 misspelling note simply loses that round's contest: since "not a misspelling of" asserts no relationship at all, Myliobatus's card contributes nothing to the grouping step this time, and the two lineages come back apart on their own the next time anything recomputes.

Nothing walks backward and nothing is un-written — the 1990 note stays on the record exactly as published. It's simply no longer the top-ranked note about what Myliobatus is.

The point: because the ledger is never anything but a recomputation, there's no separate "undo" mechanism to write or to get wrong. Reversing a claim is just adding one more note — as long as that note directly rejects the specific relationship being undone, rather than merely asserting something unrelated.
For readers who know the old system

What's actually different from Classic

The underlying philosophy — publish an opinion, let the database derive belief — carries over unchanged. What changed is how faithfully the machinery lives up to that philosophy.

Classic
2.0

A name's identity conflated "this exact spelling" with "the original combination it traces back to" — two different questions, one column.

A card is always exactly one spelling, one rank, forever. Which spelling is "original" is worked out separately, on demand.

The classification tree was rebuilt by a background daemon polling for changes every couple of seconds, with a full rebuild "weekly, to be safe." New opinions could take until the next day to show up correctly.

The ledger recomputes as part of entering the note. There's nothing to wait for overnight.

Reading a taxon's current classification could itself trigger a write to the cache — a "read" that silently mutated the database.

Reads only ever look at the ledger as it currently stands. Nothing about looking something up changes what's stored.

Undoing a synonymy or a misspelling call meant walking back through cached history to find the last clean state — logic that had to handle its own edge cases (loops, partial updates) separately from everything else.

There is no walk-back logic. A reversal is a new note; the next recompute produces the reverted state as a side effect of being correct.

Evidence was graded on a four-step scale — stated with evidence, stated without, implied, second-hand — read off a hand-written SQL CASE expression repeated in several places.

Evidence is simply yes or no: was a supporting argument given. The three middle Classic grades collapse into "no" — a deliberate simplification, with publication year doing correspondingly more of the tie-breaking work.

A rootless "belongs to" opinion — no containing group given — was effectively dropped; there was nowhere clean for it to live.

A rootless placement note is kept as a real, ranked claim: "this reference asserted no containing group," which can still win or lose against a later, better opinion.

nomen dubium, nomen nudum, and similar status calls all competed in the same reliability-ranked pool as ordinary placement and synonymy opinions — the system couldn't tell doubt about a name apart from an act that actually invalidates it.

Nomen dubium and nomen vanum are recorded but never move anything — doubt, not invalidation. Nomen nudum is the one status that can actually disqualify a card from winning its lineage's accepted spelling — and if every card in a lineage ends up disqualified this way (all nomen nudum, or all misspellings, with nothing left standing), that lineage produces no "current name" at all, rather than crowning an unavailable one by default. The same rule cascades one level up: a synonym group with no eligible member anywhere simply doesn't appear in the ledger either. Nothing is deleted — the notes are still all there — there's just nothing available to call current belief.
